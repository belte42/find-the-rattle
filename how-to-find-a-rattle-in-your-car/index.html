How to find a rattle in your car

A rattle you can only hear while driving is hard to track down: you can't take your hands off the wheel, and it stops the moment you park. This guide shows how to narrow it down, make it rattle on demand while parked, and fix it for good.

Updated 1 October 2026

The short version

  1. Note when it happens: over bumps, at a certain speed, at idle, with music, or only when it's cold.
  2. Narrow down where: empty the cabin, then listen from the passenger seat while someone else drives.
  3. Make it rattle while parked: play a slow low-frequency sweep through the car's speakers and stop when something buzzes.
  4. Find it by hand: hold that tone and press on nearby panels until the noise stops.
  5. Fix it: felt tape, a new clip, a tightened screw or a tied-down cable, then test again.

Why cars rattle

Almost every interior rattle is a loose part vibrating against something hard: a trim panel whose clips have worn, two plastic pieces rubbing at a seam, a cable tapping the back of the dashboard, or something rolling around in a door pocket.

Each of those parts has a frequency it vibrates at most easily (its resonant frequency). That's why a rattle often appears only at a certain speed, engine speed or song, and disappears when anything changes. It's also the key to finding it: play that frequency while parked and the part rattles on demand.

Step 1: Note when it happens

Before taking anything apart, spend a drive or two paying attention to when the noise appears. It narrows the list of suspects quickly.

Over bumps and rough roads

Loose trim clips, things in door pockets, the glovebox or the boot, seat belt buckles, the rear parcel shelf. A heavy clunk rather than a rattle can be suspension, so get that checked.

At one particular speed

A panel that resonates with road or wind vibration at that speed: dashboard seams, mirrors, door cards, the A-pillar trim.

At idle or certain engine revs

Engine vibration reaching the dashboard or centre console. A tinny buzz from underneath is often an exhaust heat shield instead.

With music or bass

Door cards around the speakers, the rear parcel shelf, number-plate frames, boot-lid trim. These are the easiest to find with test tones.

Only when it's cold

Plastics shrink in the cold and open up gaps at clips and seams. Dashboard seams are the usual suspects, and the noise often fades as the car warms up.

All the time, everywhere

Usually something loose in the car rather than the car itself. Empty everything (next step) before going further.

Step 2: Narrow down where

  1. Empty the car. Glovebox, door pockets, cup holders, centre console, sunglasses holder, the boot and the spare-wheel well. A surprising number of "rattles" are a pen, coins or a loose jack.
  2. Listen as a passenger. Have someone else drive on the road where it happens while you move your head around the cabin. Never go hunting for a rattle while you're driving yourself.
  3. Change one thing at a time. Hold a panel, plug in an unused seat belt, or lift out the parcel shelf, then listen again.
  4. Record it. A phone video placed in different spots makes it easier to compare which area is loudest.

You'll usually end up with an area, such as "passenger side of the dashboard" or "rear left", but not the exact part. That's where the next step comes in.

Step 3: Make it rattle while parked

Driving makes a rattle appear, but it's the worst place to find it. Because rattles are resonances, you can trigger most interior ones with sound instead: play a low-frequency tone through the car's own speakers and slowly sweep it up. When the tone hits a loose part's frequency, that part buzzes, and you're parked with both hands free.

Find The Rattle is a free web app that does exactly this. It runs in your phone's browser, with nothing to install:

  1. Park somewhere quiet and connect your phone to the car stereo over Bluetooth or aux.
  2. Start with the volume low. Raise it slowly until something starts to buzz. Louder isn't better, and very loud bass can damage speakers.
  3. Run a Rattle Test. The tone sweeps slowly through the bass range. Tap the big pad whenever you hear a rattle, and the app saves that frequency, corrected for your reaction time.
  4. Hold the tone. Freeze the sweep on a saved frequency and nudge it up or down until the rattle is loudest.

Step 4: Find it by hand

With the tone holding the rattle steady, press firmly on panels in the area one at a time. When you press the right spot, the rattle stops instantly. That's your culprit.

  • Press with a flat hand rather than a fingertip, so you cover more of each panel.
  • Work along seams, where two pieces meet, and around speaker grilles and vents.
  • If pressing one panel only makes it quieter, the culprit is probably the piece next to it.

Common culprits

Door cards

The trim panel inside each door hangs on plastic clips that loosen over time, right in front of the door speaker. Press around the speaker grille.

Dashboard seams

The dash is many pieces clipped together. Press along the seams and around the vents, screen and glovebox.

A-pillar trim

The covers either side of the windscreen. They often hide an airbag, so check your car's manual before removing them.

Seat belt buckles

An unused buckle can tap against the seat or trim. Plug it in to check.

Sunglasses holder

The flip-down box in the roof console. Empty it and press the lid shut.

Rear parcel shelf

It only rests on its supports, usually right by the rear speakers. Lift it out and test again.

Number-plate frames

They sound like they're inside the car, especially with bass. Press each plate against the car.

Door pockets and cubbies

Coins, pens and keys. Empty everything before you start.

How to fix it

  • Felt or fleece tape where two hard parts touch: trim seams, clip points, the edges of a panel. This fixes most interior rattles.
  • Replace broken clips. Pop the panel off with a plastic trim tool (not a screwdriver, which marks the plastic) and swap any snapped clips. Clips for most cars are cheap online.
  • Tighten screws holding the part that rattles.
  • Tie down loose cables behind the dash or inside the door with fleece tape and cable ties.
  • Deaden big panels. Sound-deadening pads stop large panels, such as door skins, from resonating with bass.
  • Test again with the same sweep. If it stays quiet, you're done.

When it's not an interior rattle

Test tones find rattles that respond to vibration in the cabin. Some noises have other causes and need a mechanic rather than tape:

  • Heavy clunks or knocks over bumps, which can mean worn suspension parts.
  • Noises that change when you brake or turn, or that come from the wheels.
  • A metallic rattle from underneath the car, often a loose heat shield or exhaust part.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my car only rattle at certain speeds or RPM?

Every loose part has a frequency it vibrates at most easily. At certain speeds or engine revs, the vibration reaching that part matches its frequency and it starts to rattle, then goes quiet again once you speed up or slow down.

Why does my car rattle more in cold weather?

Interior plastics shrink slightly in the cold, which opens up small gaps at clips and seams. Many rattles are worst on cold mornings and fade as the cabin warms up.

Can I find a rattle without driving?

Often, yes. Most interior rattles also respond to sound. Playing a slow low-frequency sweep through the car's speakers while parked makes loose panels buzz on demand, so you can find them by hand without driving around.

Is a rattle in my car dangerous?

Interior trim rattles are annoying but usually harmless. Metallic clunks over bumps, or noises from the wheels, brakes or underneath the car, can point to worn suspension or a loose part, so have those checked by a mechanic.

What tape stops car rattles?

Felt or fleece tape works best where two hard surfaces touch, such as trim seams and clips. Foam tape fills larger gaps, and fleece-wrapped cable ties stop loose wiring from tapping against panels.
